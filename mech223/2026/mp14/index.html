1. Mixed triple product (Sec. 3.2C)

Theme: understanding the mixed triple product \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F})\).

The mixed triple product (or scalar triple product) gives the moment of a force about a line (an axis), not just a point. It is a scalar:

\[ M_{OL}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F})= \begin{vmatrix}\lambda_x & \lambda_y & \lambda_z\\ r_x & r_y & r_z\\ F_x & F_y & F_z\end{vmatrix} \]
  • Row 1: \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is the unit vector along the line \(OL\). (Divide the direction vector by its magnitude first.)
  • Row 2: \(\mathbf{r}\) is the position vector from any point on the line to any point on the line of action of \(\mathbf{F}\).
  • Row 3: \(\mathbf{F}\) is the force in component form.

The result \(M_{OL}\) is a scalar. It is the signed component of \(\mathbf{M}_O\) along \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\); \(|M_{OL}|\) is its magnitude. Its sign tells the sense: positive means the moment points along \(+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\); negative means opposite.

Key insight: The mixed triple product is the dot product of \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) with the full moment vector \(\mathbf{M}_O=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\). It projects the 3D moment onto the axis direction \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). Think of it as "how much of the moment is along this axis?"

Worked example

A line runs from \(O(0,0,0)\) to \(A(3,0,4)\) m. A force \(\mathbf{F}=(100\,\mathbf{i}+50\,\mathbf{j}-80\,\mathbf{k})\) N acts at point \(B(0,2,0)\) m. Find the moment of \(\mathbf{F}\) about line \(OA\).

  1. Direction vector of line \(OA\): \(\overrightarrow{OA}=(3,0,4)\) m, length \(\sqrt{9+16}=5\) m.
  2. Unit vector: \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(3/5,\,0,\,4/5)=(0.6,\,0,\,0.8)\).
  3. Position vector from \(O\) (a point on the line) to \(B\) (a point on the line of action of \(\mathbf{F}\)): \(\mathbf{r}=(0,2,0)\) m.
  4. Mixed triple product:
    \[ M_{OA}=\begin{vmatrix}0.6 & 0 & 0.8\\ 0 & 2 & 0\\ 100 & 50 & -80\end{vmatrix} =0.6[(2)(-80)-0]-0+0.8[(0)(50)-(2)(100)]=-96-160=-256\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

The moment is \(-256\) N·m. The negative sign means the moment points from \(A\) toward \(O\) (opposite \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\)).

Check: Compute \(\mathbf{M}_O=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\) first: \(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}=[(2)(-80)-0]\,\mathbf{i}-[(0)(-80)-0]\,\mathbf{j}+[(0)(50)-(2)(100)]\,\mathbf{k}=(-160\,\mathbf{i}+0\,\mathbf{j}-200\,\mathbf{k})\) N·m. Then \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{M}_O=(0.6)(-160)+0+(0.8)(-200)=-96-160=-256\) N·m. \(\checkmark\)

Common mistake: dropping the \(\mathbf{k}\) term of \(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\) gives \(-96\) N·m, which does not match. Always expand all three components.

Self-check: A line runs along the \(z\)-axis. \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0,0,1)\). A force \(\mathbf{F}=(20\,\mathbf{i}+30\,\mathbf{j})\) N acts at \(C(1,0,0)\) m. Find \(M_{OL}\).

Answer: self-check 1 (moment about z-axis)\(30\) N·m. (\(\mathbf{r}=(1,0,0)\), \(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}=(0\,\mathbf{i}-0\,\mathbf{j}+30\,\mathbf{k})\) N·m, \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{M}_O=(0)(0)+(0)(0)+(1)(30)=30\) N·m.)

2. Scalar vs. vector analysis (Sec. 3.2C)

Theme: why scalar analysis uses only one axis, and how it differs from vector analysis.

Vector analysis gives the full moment vector in 3D:

\[ \mathbf{M}_O=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}=(M_x\,\mathbf{i}+M_y\,\mathbf{j}+M_z\,\mathbf{k})\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

This has three components: \(M_x\), \(M_y\), \(M_z\). You need all three to describe the moment completely.

Scalar analysis gives the moment component along one axis (a line \(OL\)):

\[ M_{OL}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}) \]

This is a single scalar. It tells you how much of the moment is along the direction \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). The other two perpendicular components are ignored.

When to use each method: Use scalar analysis when the problem asks for the moment about a specific axis (for example, "Find the moment about the \(z\)-axis" or "Find the moment about line \(AB\)"). Use vector analysis when you need the complete 3D moment vector, or when you will sum moments in all three directions for equilibrium.

Why only one axis matters in scalar analysis

The mixed triple product \(M_{OL}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F})\) is the projection of the full moment \(\mathbf{M}_O\) onto the unit vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). A dot product picks out the component along one direction. That is why "only one axis is relevant" — you are asking for the moment about that axis, not the full 3D moment.

Worked example

A force \(\mathbf{F}=(60\,\mathbf{i}-40\,\mathbf{j}+20\,\mathbf{k})\) N acts at \(D(2,1,3)\) m. Find (a) the full moment about the origin, (b) the component along the \(y\)-axis.

(a) Vector analysis: \(\mathbf{r}=(2,1,3)\) m.

\[ \mathbf{M}_O=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}= \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\ 2 & 1 & 3\\ 60 & -40 & 20\end{vmatrix} =[(1)(20)-(3)(-40)]\,\mathbf{i}-[(2)(20)-(3)(60)]\,\mathbf{j}+[(2)(-40)-(1)(60)]\,\mathbf{k} \]
\[ =(20+120)\,\mathbf{i}-(40-180)\,\mathbf{j}+(-80-60)\,\mathbf{k}=(140\,\mathbf{i}+140\,\mathbf{j}-140\,\mathbf{k})\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

(b) Scalar analysis (moment about \(y\)-axis): \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0,1,0)\).

\[ M_{y}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{M}_O=(0)(140)+(1)(140)+(0)(-140)=140\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

Or directly:

\[ M_{y}=\begin{vmatrix}0 & 1 & 0\\ 2 & 1 & 3\\ 60 & -40 & 20\end{vmatrix}=0-1[(2)(20)-(3)(60)]+0=-(40-180)=140\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

Same answer. The scalar method picks out the \(y\)-component \(M_y=140\) N·m from the full vector \(\mathbf{M}_O\).

Self-check: For the same force and point, find the moment component about the \(x\)-axis.

Answer: self-check 2 (moment about x-axis)\(140\) N·m. (\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(1,0,0)\), \(M_x=(1)(140)+(0)(140)+(0)(-140)=140\) N·m.)

3. Clockwise/counterclockwise by looking down (Sec. 3.2C)

Theme: determining sense of rotation by looking down from a point.

When you find the moment of a force about a line, the sign of \(M_{OL}\) tells you the sense of rotation about that line. To visualize the sense, use the right-hand rule:

  1. Point your right thumb along the unit vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) (the positive direction of the line).
  2. Your fingers curl in the direction of positive rotation (counterclockwise when \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) points toward you, i.e., looking from its tip back toward the line).
  3. If \(M_{OL}>0\), the moment is in the positive sense (counterclockwise as seen with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) pointing toward you).
  4. If \(M_{OL}<0\), the moment is in the negative sense (clockwise as seen with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) pointing toward you).

The "looking down" rule: Stand at the tip of \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) and look back along the line, so \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) points toward you. For \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=+\mathbf{k}\), that means looking down from above onto the \(xy\)-plane. If the force would turn the body counterclockwise from that viewpoint, \(M_{OL}>0\); if clockwise, \(M_{OL}<0\). This is the same as the right-hand rule: thumb along \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), fingers curl counterclockwise when \(M_{OL}>0\).

Worked example

A vertical line runs upward along the \(z\)-axis. \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0,0,1)\). A horizontal force \(\mathbf{F}=(50\,\mathbf{i})\) N acts at \(P(2,0,1)\) m. First find the moment about the \(z\)-axis, then repeat with a second force and determine the sense of rotation.

Position vector from origin (a point on the \(z\)-axis) to \(P\): \(\mathbf{r}=(2,0,1)\) m.

\[ M_z=\begin{vmatrix}0 & 0 & 1\\ 2 & 0 & 1\\ 50 & 0 & 0\end{vmatrix} =0-0+1[(2)(0)-(0)(50)]=0\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

The moment is zero: this force points straight away from the \(z\)-axis (it is radial), so it cannot turn the body about that axis. Now take a force that is not radial.

Try \(\mathbf{F}=(0,50,0)\) N (force in the \(+y\)-direction) at \(P(2,0,1)\) m.

\[ M_z=\begin{vmatrix}0 & 0 & 1\\ 2 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 50 & 0\end{vmatrix} =0-0+1[(2)(50)-(0)(0)]=100\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

The moment is \(+100\) N·m. The positive sign means the moment is counterclockwise when viewed from the tip of \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=+\mathbf{k}\) looking back (from above, looking down at the \(xy\)-plane). Visual check: the force points in the \(+y\)-direction at a point \(x=2\) (to the right of the \(z\)-axis). This would rotate the body counterclockwise (like opening a door). \(\checkmark\)

Self-check: Same setup, but force \(\mathbf{F}=(50,0,0)\) N at \(P(0,2,1)\) m. Find \(M_z\) and the sense.

Answer: self-check 3 (sense about z-axis)\(-100\) N·m, clockwise. (\(\mathbf{r}=(0,2,1)\), \(M_z=[(0)(0)-(2)(50)]=-100\) N·m. Force in \(+x\)-direction at \(y=2\) rotates clockwise when looking down from \(+z\).)

4. Finding force / remembering moment steps (Sec. 3.1, 3.2)

Theme: systematic steps for calculating moments.

Follow this sequence for moment problems:

Step-by-step moment recipe:

  1. Identify the moment center or axis. Is the problem asking for the moment about a point \(O\) (vector) or about a line \(OL\) (scalar)?
  2. Write the force in component form. If the force magnitude and direction are given, decompose: \(\mathbf{F}=F(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{i}+\sin\theta\,\mathbf{j})\) or use unit vectors.
  3. Write the position vector \(\mathbf{r}\). From the moment center (or any point on the moment axis) to any point on the line of action of the force. Use coordinates: \(\mathbf{r}=(x_F-x_O)\,\mathbf{i}+(y_F-y_O)\,\mathbf{j}+(z_F-z_O)\,\mathbf{k}\).
  4. Compute the moment.
    • For moment about a point: \(\mathbf{M}_O=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\) (3×3 determinant).
    • For moment about a line: \(M_{OL}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F})\) (3×3 determinant with \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) in row 1).
  5. Check units and sign. Moment has units N·m. Sign tells rotation sense.

Worked example: full problem

A beam lies along the \(x\)-axis from \(A(0,0,0)\) to \(B(4,0,0)\) m. A cable pulls with force \(F=200\) N at point \(C(3,0,0)\) m. The cable runs from \(C\) to \(D(3,2,1)\) m. Find the moment of the cable force about point \(A\).

  1. Moment center: \(A(0,0,0)\).
  2. Force direction: Cable runs from \(C(3,0,0)\) to \(D(3,2,1)\). Direction vector: \(\overrightarrow{CD}=(0,2,1)\), length \(\sqrt{0+4+1}=\sqrt{5}\). Unit vector: \(\mathbf{u}=(0,\,2/\sqrt{5},\,1/\sqrt{5})\). Force: \(\mathbf{F}=200\mathbf{u}=(0,\,400/\sqrt{5},\,200/\sqrt{5})\approx(0,\,178.89,\,89.44)\) N.
  3. Position vector: From \(A\) to \(C\): \(\mathbf{r}=(3,0,0)\) m.
  4. Cross product:
    \[ \mathbf{M}_A=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}= \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\ 3 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 178.89 & 89.44\end{vmatrix} =[(0)(89.44)-0]\,\mathbf{i}-[(3)(89.44)-0]\,\mathbf{j}+[(3)(178.89)-0]\,\mathbf{k} \]
    \[ =(0\,\mathbf{i}-268.3\,\mathbf{j}+536.7\,\mathbf{k})\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]
  5. Exact (keeping fractions): \(\mathbf{F}=(0,\,400/\sqrt{5},\,200/\sqrt{5})\) N, so \(M_y=-(3)(200/\sqrt{5})=-600/\sqrt{5}=-268.3\) N·m, \(M_z=(3)(400/\sqrt{5})=1200/\sqrt{5}=536.7\) N·m.

Magnitude: \(|\mathbf{M}_A|=\sqrt{0+268.3^2+536.7^2}\approx 600\) N·m.

Self-check: Same problem, but find the moment about the \(x\)-axis (the beam axis).

Answer: self-check 4 (moment about x-axis)\(0\) N·m. (\(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(1,0,0)\), \(M_x=(1)(0)+(0)(-268.3)+(0)(536.7)=0\). The cable's line of action passes through point \(C\) on the beam (\(x\)) axis, so it intersects the axis and produces no moment about it.)

5. Moments about a new point or axis (Sec. 3.1–3.2)

Theme: why we need to recalculate moments for a new point or axis when we already have Cartesian components.

The Cartesian components of the moment \(\mathbf{M}_O=(M_x,M_y,M_z)\) are valid about the specific point \(O\). If you want the moment about a different point \(P\), you must recalculate using \(\mathbf{M}_P=\mathbf{r}_P\times\mathbf{F}\), where \(\mathbf{r}_P\) is the position vector from \(P\) to any point on the line of action of the force.

The moment of a force depends on the moment center. Moving the moment center changes the position vector \(\mathbf{r}\), which changes the moment.

Key insight: The moment of a force about a point is not a free vector; it is tied to that point. (A couple moment is the exception: it is a free vector, the same about every point.) The moment \(\mathbf{M}_O\) describes the rotational effect about point \(O\). If you move to a different point \(P\), the rotational effect changes. The Cartesian components \((M_x,M_y,M_z)\) are projections of the moment onto the coordinate axes, but those components are about point \(O\), not universal.

When moments are the same at different points

If a force acts at the same distance from two points (measured perpendicular to its line of action), the moment magnitude is the same. But the moment direction may differ. In general, \(\mathbf{M}_P\neq\mathbf{M}_O\) unless the two moment centers lie on a line parallel to \(\mathbf{F}\) (i.e., \(\mathbf{r}_P-\mathbf{r}_O\parallel\mathbf{F}\)). A couple moment, by contrast, is the same about every point.

The mixed triple product shortcut

The mixed triple product \(M_{OL}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F})\) lets you find the moment component along any axis \(OL\) without first computing the full moment \(\mathbf{M}_O\) in a different coordinate system. You pick the axis direction \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), and the triple product projects the moment onto that axis in one step. Each rectangular component is itself a moment about an axis: \(M_x\) is the turning effect about the \(x\)-axis (rotation in the \(yz\)-plane), and so on. Projecting onto a new axis answers a new question without redoing the whole problem.

Worked example: moment at two different points

A force \(\mathbf{F}=(0,0,-100)\) N acts at \(B(3,2,5)\) m. Find the moment about (a) the origin \(O(0,0,0)\), (b) point \(Q(1,0,0)\) m.

(a) Moment about \(O\): \(\mathbf{r}_O=(3,2,5)\) m.

\[ \mathbf{M}_O=\mathbf{r}_O\times\mathbf{F}= \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\ 3 & 2 & 5\\ 0 & 0 & -100\end{vmatrix} =[(2)(-100)-0]\,\mathbf{i}-[(3)(-100)-0]\,\mathbf{j}+0\,\mathbf{k}=(-200\,\mathbf{i}+300\,\mathbf{j})\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

(b) Moment about \(Q\): \(\mathbf{r}_Q=(3-1,\,2-0,\,5-0)=(2,2,5)\) m.

\[ \mathbf{M}_Q=\mathbf{r}_Q\times\mathbf{F}= \begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k}\\ 2 & 2 & 5\\ 0 & 0 & -100\end{vmatrix} =[(2)(-100)-0]\,\mathbf{i}-[(2)(-100)-0]\,\mathbf{j}+0\,\mathbf{k}=(-200\,\mathbf{i}+200\,\mathbf{j})\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

The two moments are different: \(\mathbf{M}_O\neq\mathbf{M}_Q\). The \(y\)-components differ because \(M_y=-r_xF_z\) here, and \(r_x\) is measured from the moment center: it is 3 m from \(O\) but 2 m from \(Q\), so \(M_y\) drops from 300 to 200 N·m. (\(M_y\) about \(Q\) is the moment about the line through \(Q\) parallel to \(y\).)

Self-check: For the same force and point \(B\), find the moment about \(R(3,0,0)\) m.

Answer: self-check 5 (moment about R)\(\mathbf{M}_R=(-200\,\mathbf{i})\) N·m. (\(\mathbf{r}_R=(3-3,\,2-0,\,5-0)=(0,2,5)\) m, \(\mathbf{F}=(0,0,-100)\) N: \(M_x=(2)(-100)-(5)(0)=-200\), \(M_y=-[(0)(-100)-(5)(0)]=0\), \(M_z=(0)(0)-(2)(0)=0\).)

6. Unit vectors, projections, signs, equations (Sec. 3.1–3.2)

Theme: understanding unit vectors, dot products for projections, sign conventions, and when to use which equation.

These are interconnected tools for working with 3D vectors and moments:

  • Unit vectors \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\): A unit vector has magnitude 1 and points in a direction. To find it, divide a direction vector by its length: \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{v}/|\mathbf{v}|\). Unit vectors give direction without scaling.
  • Projections (dot product): The dot product \(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}=|\mathbf{A}||\mathbf{B}|\cos\theta\) gives the component of \(\mathbf{A}\) along \(\mathbf{B}\). If \(\mathbf{B}\) is a unit vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), then \(\mathbf{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is the scalar component of \(\mathbf{A}\) in the \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) direction.
  • Signs: The sign of a dot product tells you if vectors point in the same direction (positive) or opposite (negative). The sign of a cross product component tells you the sense of rotation (right-hand rule).
  • Which equation to use:
    • For moment about a point: \(\mathbf{M}_O=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}\) (vector, 3 components).
    • For moment about a line/axis: \(M_{OL}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F})\) (scalar, one component).
    • For perpendicular distance (2D): \(M_O=Fd\) (scalar magnitude).

Practice tip: Start every problem by identifying what you know (coordinates, forces) and what you need (moment about a point or a line). Write the vectors in component form. Use the cross product for moments, the dot product for projections. The formulas are always the same; the challenge is setting up \(\mathbf{r}\), \(\mathbf{F}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) correctly.

Worked example: unit vector as a vector

A line runs from \(P(1,2,2)\) to \(Q(4,6,2)\) m. Find the unit vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) along \(PQ\).

  1. Direction vector: \(\overrightarrow{PQ}=(4-1,\,6-2,\,2-2)=(3,4,0)\) m.
  2. Magnitude: \(|\overrightarrow{PQ}|=\sqrt{9+16+0}=5\) m.
  3. Unit vector: \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(3/5,\,4/5,\,0)=(0.6,\,0.8,\,0)\).

Check: \(|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|=\sqrt{0.36+0.64+0}=1\,\checkmark\). The unit vector is itself a vector (it has components and direction), but its magnitude is 1.

Worked example: projection

A force \(\mathbf{F}=(10,20,30)\) N. Find the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) in the direction \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(0.6,0.8,0)\).

\[ F_{\lambda}=\mathbf{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(10)(0.6)+(20)(0.8)+(30)(0)=6+16=22\ \text{N} \]

The component is 22 N. This is the part of \(\mathbf{F}\) that lies along the direction \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). The projection is a scalar (with sign).

Self-check: Same force, find the magnitude of the component perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\).

Answer: self-check 6 (perpendicular component)Use \(F_{\perp}=\sqrt{|\mathbf{F}|^2-F_{\lambda}^2}\). \(|\mathbf{F}|=\sqrt{100+400+900}=\sqrt{1400}\approx 37.4\) N. \(F_{\lambda}=22\) N. \(F_{\perp}=\sqrt{1400-484}=\sqrt{916}\approx 30.3\) N.

7. Homework difficulty

Theme: homework problems feel harder than lecture examples.

Homework problems often combine several lecture concepts in one problem: finding a force from cable geometry, computing moments about multiple axes, checking equilibrium, or working in 3D with angled forces. Each individual step matches a lecture example, but the combination can feel overwhelming.

Strategy: Break each homework problem into the lecture steps: (1) find all forces in component form, (2) pick a moment center, (3) compute each moment, (4) sum for equilibrium. Write every step. If a specific homework question is unclear, bring the problem number to office hours (times posted in the Office Hours announcement on Canvas) or ask on Canvas discussion.

Recitation examples also bridge lecture to homework. Practice the homework problems early, and use the midweek class time to ask about the ones that are confusing.
