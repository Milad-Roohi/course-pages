1. Where to cut in the method of sections

“I was confused on how to determine where to cut the truss when using the method of sectioning.”

“Today's lecture was pretty good and easy to understand. The one part that I felt like had me stuck would be when analyzing trusts determining where one should place the cut on their diagram.”

“The muddiest point was how, after cutting a truss, we found the value in kips for a beam using a moment, and for another beam we didn't.”

Start from the member you were asked about, because the cut has to pass through it. Then slice the truss (trusses, plural) completely in two, crossing as few other members as you can. Three unknown members is the limit, because one piece of the truss gives you three equilibrium equations.

Placing the cut

  1. Name the member force you want. The cut goes through that member.
  2. Carry the cut all the way across so the truss separates into two pieces. It does not have to be a straight line.
  3. Cross no more than three members whose forces are unknown.
  4. Keep the piece with fewer loads and reactions on it. Either piece gives the same answers.
  5. On the piece you keep, draw each cut member as a force pulling away from the piece (tension). A negative answer means compression.

After the cut: moment or force sum?

A moment equation is a way to make two of the three unknowns disappear. To find one cut member, look at where the lines of the other two cut members cross and take moments there. Both of them pass through that point, so neither appears in the equation.

That trick needs a crossing point. When the other two cut members are parallel, as the top and bottom chords are here, their lines never meet. So for the third member you sum forces perpendicular to the parallel pair instead, and those two drop out of that equation. That is why one member came from a moment and another did not.

Worked example: forces in \(FG\), \(BC\), and \(FC\)

All three members sit in the panel between \(B\) and \(C\), so cut vertically through that panel. The cut crosses \(FG\), \(FC\), and \(BC\): three unknowns. Keep the left piece (joints \(A\), \(B\), \(F\)). The forces on it are \(A_x = 8\) kips left, \(A_y = 9\) kips up, the 6-kip load at \(B\), and the three cut-member forces, each drawn as tension. Take counterclockwise as positive.

\(FG\): moments about \(C\), where \(FC\) and \(BC\) cross. \(A_x\) also passes through \(C\).

\[ \sum M_C = -(9)(16) + (6)(8) - F_{FG}(6) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{FG} = -16\ \text{kips} \quad (16\ \text{kips, compression}) \]

\(BC\): moments about \(F\), where \(FG\) and \(FC\) cross. The 6-kip load is directly below \(F\), so it has no moment arm.

\[ \sum M_F = -(9)(8) - (8)(6) + F_{BC}(6) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{BC} = 20\ \text{kips} \quad (\text{tension}) \]

\(FC\): no moment this time. The other two cut members, \(FG\) and \(BC\), are both horizontal and never cross. They also have no vertical component, so sum forces vertically. \(FC\) pulls on the left piece down and to the right along its 3-4-5 slope.

\[ \sum F_y = 9 - 6 - \tfrac{3}{5}F_{FC} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{FC} = 5\ \text{kips} \quad (\text{tension}) \]

The equation not yet used is the check:

\[ \sum F_x = -8 + (-16) + 20 + \tfrac{4}{5}(5) = 0 \ \checkmark \]

Self-check: Find the forces in \(GH\), \(CH\), and \(CD\). Where do you cut, and which piece do you keep?

Answer

Cut through the panel between \(C\) and \(D\) and keep the right piece (joints \(D\), \(E\), \(H\)), which carries only \(E_y\) and the 8-kip load. Moments about \(C\): \((9)(16) - (8)(6) + F_{GH}(6) = 0\), so \(F_{GH} = -16\) kips (16 kips, compression). Moments about \(H\): \((9)(8) - F_{CD}(6) = 0\), so \(F_{CD} = 12\) kips (tension). The chords are parallel, so use \(\sum F_y = 9 - \tfrac{3}{5}F_{CH} = 0\), giving \(F_{CH} = 15\) kips (tension).

2. \(\sum F\) or \(\sum M\) for truss reactions?

“When finding the reactions in a truss when do we find the sum of forces and when do we find the sum of the moment? We use both to solve for the different forces correct?”

Correct, you use both. For reactions, the whole truss is one rigid body with three equations: \(\sum F_x = 0\), \(\sum F_y = 0\), and \(\sum M = 0\). A pin and a roller give three unknowns, so each equation has a job. What you choose is the order, and the best first move is usually a moment equation about the pin.

A reliable order

  1. \(\sum M = 0\) about the pin. Both pin components pass through it, so the roller reaction is the only unknown.
  2. \(\sum F_y = 0\) for the vertical pin component.
  3. \(\sum F_x = 0\) for the horizontal pin component.
  4. Check with \(\sum M = 0\) about a different point.

Worked example: reactions for the truss above

Draw the whole truss as one body. Assume \(A_x\) to the right, and \(A_y\) and \(E_y\) up. Counterclockwise is positive. The 8-kip load at \(H\) acts 6 ft above \(A\) and turns the truss clockwise about \(A\).

\[ \sum M_A = E_y(32) - (6)(8) - (12)(16) - (8)(6) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad E_y = \frac{288}{32} = 9\ \text{kips} \]
\[ \sum F_y = A_y + 9 - 6 - 12 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_y = 9\ \text{kips} \]
\[ \sum F_x = A_x + 8 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_x = -8\ \text{kips} \quad (8\ \text{kips to the left}) \]

Check with moments about \(E\), which was not used:

\[ \sum M_E = -(9)(32) + (6)(24) + (12)(16) - (8)(6) = 0 \ \checkmark \]

The same choice at each stage. Whole truss: three equations, and a moment about the pin comes first. A cut piece: three equations, with a moment about the crossing point of two cut members when there is one (Section 1). A single joint: only \(\sum F_x\) and \(\sum F_y\), because every force passes through the joint and a moment equation there says nothing.

Self-check: Remove the 8-kip load at \(H\) and keep the two vertical loads. What are the reactions?

Answer

\(\sum M_A = E_y(32) - (6)(8) - (12)(16) = 0\) gives \(E_y = 7.5\) kips. Then \(A_y = 18 - 7.5 = 10.5\) kips and \(A_x = 0\).

3. Assumed directions at joints

“I found it hard to keep track of the directions of the forces. We're looking at the forces acting on a rigid body and then the reactions of that right? For members, are we just to assume the direction of the reaction force at each joint?”

Yes, you assume, and the sign of the answer tells you whether you guessed right. On the whole truss, the forces are the applied loads and the support reactions. Inside the truss, each member pulls or pushes on the joint at each of its ends, and the joint acts back on the member with an equal and opposite force.

One habit removes most of the bookkeeping: draw every unknown member force as tension, an arrow leaving the joint along the member. A positive answer is tension. A negative answer is compression. When you move to the next joint, draw that member as tension again and carry the signed value into the equations.

Worked example: joints \(A\), \(B\), and \(F\)

Joint \(A\). Forces: the reactions (8 kips left, 9 kips up), \(F_{AB}\) to the right, and \(F_{AF}\) up and to the right along the 3-4-5 slope.

\[ \sum F_y = 9 + \tfrac{3}{5}F_{AF} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{AF} = -15\ \text{kips} \quad (15\ \text{kips, compression}) \]
\[ \sum F_x = -8 + F_{AB} + \tfrac{4}{5}(-15) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{AB} = 20\ \text{kips} \quad (\text{tension}) \]

The tension guess for \(AF\) was wrong, and the minus sign reported it. Nothing needs redrawing.

Joint \(B\). Vertically there are only \(F_{BF}\) up and the 6-kip load down, so \(F_{BF} = 6\) kips (tension). Horizontally, \(F_{BC} = F_{AB} = 20\) kips (tension).

Joint \(F\). Draw all four members as tension: \(AF\) down-left, \(BF\) straight down, \(FC\) down-right, \(FG\) to the right. Substitute \(F_{AF} = -15\) with its sign.

\[ \sum F_y = -\tfrac{3}{5}(-15) - 6 - \tfrac{3}{5}F_{FC} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{FC} = 5\ \text{kips} \quad (\text{tension}) \]
\[ \sum F_x = -\tfrac{4}{5}(-15) + \tfrac{4}{5}(5) + F_{FG} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{FG} = -16\ \text{kips} \quad (16\ \text{kips, compression}) \]

These are the same \(F_{BC}\), \(F_{FC}\), and \(F_{FG}\) the section cut gave in Section 1. Joints and sections are two routes to the same forces.

Reading the arrows. A member in tension pulls on the joints at both of its ends. A member in compression pushes on both. If you prefer to redraw a compression member with its arrow pointing into the joint, use the positive magnitude from then on. Pick one of the two habits and do not mix them within a problem.

Self-check: Solve joint \(E\) for \(F_{HE}\) and \(F_{DE}\).

Answer

With both drawn as tension: \(\sum F_y = 9 + \tfrac{3}{5}F_{HE} = 0\) gives \(F_{HE} = -15\) kips (15 kips, compression). \(\sum F_x = -F_{DE} - \tfrac{4}{5}(-15) = 0\) gives \(F_{DE} = 12\) kips (tension).

4. Two-force members, truss members, and frames

“The muddiest point was what 2 force member is compared to a truss member is, and are these the only two types of members that can be within a frame.”

“The way frames work was a little muddy”

A truss member and a two-force member are not two different things: every truss member is a two-force member. The pair to compare is two-force and multi-force, and counting where forces act on a member tells you which one you have.

  • Two-force member. Forces act at only two points, the end connections. For equilibrium those two forces must be equal, opposite, and along the line joining the two points. A straight truss member is exactly this, so its force runs along the member: tension or compression, one unknown.
  • Multi-force member. Forces act at three or more points, and they are not all concurrent or collinear. A pin on a multi-force member generally carries two unknown components.
  • Truss. Every member is a two-force member.
  • Frame. At least one member is a multi-force member. A frame can still contain two-force members, and they still carry only a collinear pair of forces.

So yes: for the pin-connected structures in this course, each member of a frame is one or the other. Find the two-force members first, because each one turns a two-component pin force into a single unknown with a known line of action. Wednesday introduced these categories. Full frame analysis, member by member, comes later in the term.

Worked example: one frame, both kinds of member

Horizontal beam \(ABC\) is 6 ft long and pinned to a wall at \(A\). Strut \(BD\) is pinned to the beam at \(B\), 4 ft from \(A\), and to the wall at \(D\), 3 ft directly below \(A\). A 300-lb load acts downward at the free end \(C\). Neglect the weights of the members.

  • Strut \(BD\): forces at its two end pins only, so it is a two-force member. Its force acts along the line \(DB\), a 3-4-5 slope (4 ft across, 3 ft up, 5 ft long).
  • Beam \(ABC\): forces at \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\), so it is a multi-force member. The pin at \(A\) has two components, \(A_x\) and \(A_y\).

Draw the beam alone. Assume the strut pushes on it at \(B\), up and to the right, with magnitude \(F_{BD}\). Assume \(A_x\) to the right and \(A_y\) up.

\[ \sum M_A = \tfrac{3}{5}F_{BD}(4) - (300)(6) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{BD} = 750\ \text{lb} \quad (\text{compression}) \]
\[ \sum F_x = A_x + \tfrac{4}{5}(750) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_x = -600\ \text{lb} \quad (600\ \text{lb to the left}) \]
\[ \sum F_y = A_y + \tfrac{3}{5}(750) - 300 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad A_y = -150\ \text{lb} \quad (150\ \text{lb down}) \]

Two things to notice. Treating \(BD\) as a two-force member left three unknowns for three equations; with separate \(B_x\) and \(B_y\) the beam alone would have had four. And the force at \(A\) (600 lb left, 150 lb down) does not point along the beam, which is what a multi-force member looks like.

Self-check: Change the load at \(C\) to 200 lb. Find \(F_{BD}\), \(A_x\), and \(A_y\).

Answer

\(F_{BD} = (200)(6)\big/\big(\tfrac{3}{5}\cdot 4\big) = 500\) lb (compression). \(A_x = -400\) lb (400 lb to the left). \(A_y = 200 - 300 = -100\) lb (100 lb down).

5. Writing the equations for the forces

“The lecture today was good. I am struggling a little bit with figuring out the equations for the forces, but with practice I hope to get a better understanding.”

Every equation on this page came from the same place: a free-body diagram and \(\sum F_x = 0\), \(\sum F_y = 0\), \(\sum M = 0\). What changes from step to step is only what you isolate.

Which free-body diagram gives which equations
What you isolateEquations you getWhat you solve for
Whole truss\(\sum F_x\), \(\sum F_y\), \(\sum M\)Support reactions (up to three)
One joint\(\sum F_x\), \(\sum F_y\)Up to two member forces
One piece after a cut\(\sum F_x\), \(\sum F_y\), \(\sum M\)Up to three cut-member forces

For an inclined member, take the components from the member's own geometry. If it runs \(a\) across and \(b\) up, its length is \(L = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\) and

\[ F_x = F\,\frac{a}{L}, \qquad F_y = F\,\frac{b}{L} \]

On this page \(a = 8\), \(b = 6\), \(L = 10\), which is where every \(\tfrac{4}{5}\) and \(\tfrac{3}{5}\) came from. No angle is needed.

Practice is the right plan. A good first set: finish the example truss on your own and compare with the full list.

6. Waiting for it to get hard

“i think this is making sense but im waiting for the moment it randomly gets so hard i get lost”

It making sense now is real evidence, so keep it. And the jump you are bracing for is rarely random. The method of sections and, later, frames use the same three equations you already trust. What grows is the number of arrows on the page and the number of choices about what to isolate.

When a problem does feel like a wall, the cause is usually one step back: a free-body diagram missing a force, or an equation written before the directions were settled. That gives you something specific to do.

  • Work two or three problems on several days of the week, and not all of them the night before they are due.
  • Before any equation, say which body you isolated and count its unknowns.
  • End each problem with the unused equation as a check, as in Sections 1 and 2.
  • Ask at the first step that feels like guessing: office hours, Canvas, or recitation with Efe. A small question now is easier than a large one in two weeks.
