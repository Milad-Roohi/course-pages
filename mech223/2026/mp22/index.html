1. Where forces act

Every equilibrium equation ties a force to a line of action and, for moments, to a perpendicular distance from that line to your reference point. In this week’s truss work, the main locations are straightforward once the diagram is labeled.

  • Applied loads act at the point shown (often a joint where members meet).
  • Support reactions act at the support (pin, roller, or link) as drawn on the truss.
  • Member forces at a joint act at that joint, directed along the member.
  • A two-force member has forces only at its two ends; those two forces are equal, opposite, and collinear along the member.

For a moment about point \(O\), use the shortest distance from \(O\) to the line of action:

\[ M_O = F\,d_\perp \]

where \(d_\perp\) is perpendicular to the force’s line of action, not along the member unless the geometry makes those the same.

Distributed load reminder (only if it appears on your diagram): a uniform load over length \(L\) can be replaced by a single force equal to the total load, acting at the centroid (\(L/2\) from one end for a uniform rectangle). That is a location rule, not the focus of this week’s joint method.

Worked example: locating actions and a moment arm

Joint \(B\) is 2.0 m to the right of pin \(A\) and at the same elevation. A horizontal member carries a 180 N force drawn at \(B\) pointing to the right (along the top chord). Find the moment of this force about \(A\).

The line of action is the horizontal line through \(B\) (\(y = 0\)). Point \(A\) lies on that line, so \(d_\perp = 0\) and

\[ M_A = 0 \]

Now suppose the same 180 N horizontal force acts at joint \(C\), which is 2.0 m to the right of \(B\) and 1.5 m above the chord. The line of action is still horizontal, now through elevation \(y = 1.5\) m. From \(A\), the perpendicular distance to that line is 1.5 m:

\[ M_A = (180\ \text{N})(1.5\ \text{m}) = 270\ \text{N}{\cdot}\text{m} \]

Sign depends on your choice of positive rotation; magnitude is what confirms whether the moment is zero or not.

Self-check: A 120 N downward load is applied at joint \(D\) directly above \(A\), 1.2 m higher. What is \(d_\perp\) for \(\sum M_A\) using that vertical force?

Answer\(d_\perp = 0\) because the line of action passes through \(A\), so the load produces no moment about \(A\) (only a vertical force in \(\sum F_y\)).

2. Tracking force directions

Truss analysis is bookkeeping on top of Newton’s laws. Pick a consistent \(+x\) and \(+y\) on every figure. For each unknown, draw an arrow in an assumed direction. For a member, assume tension (arrow pulls the joint along the member) or compression; a negative algebraic result flips the actual sense. At a joint, the force the member exerts on the joint is opposite to the force the joint exerts on the member.

Method of joints — minimal workflow

  1. Solve global equilibrium for external reactions if needed.
  2. Isolate one joint with at most two unknown member forces (when possible).
  3. Draw all member forces on the joint FBD before writing equations.
  4. Write \(\sum F_x = 0\), \(\sum F_y = 0\).
  5. Move to the next joint; reuse solved member forces with opposite direction on the adjacent joint.

Worked example: symmetric joint with a downward load

At joint \(J\), two identical members meet and slope upward at \(60^\circ\) above the horizontal (one toward each support). A 500 N load acts downward at \(J\). Assume both members are in tension as drawn (each pulls on \(J\) toward its support). By symmetry the member forces have equal magnitude \(F\).

Each tensile force has an upward vertical component \(F\sin 60^\circ\). With \(+y\) upward:

\[ \sum F_y = 2F\sin 60^\circ - 500\ \text{N} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = \frac{500}{2\sin 60^\circ} \approx 288.7\ \text{N}\ (\text{tension}) \]

\(\sum F_x = 0\) is satisfied automatically for this symmetric layout. Had you assumed compression and obtained a negative \(F\), you would switch the arrow direction and report compression with the positive magnitude.

Self-check: A joint has two members sloping upward at \(45^\circ\) to the horizontal (symmetric) and a 400 N downward load. With the same tension assumption, what is \(F\) in each member?

Answer\(F = 400/(2\sin 45^\circ) \approx 283\) N tension.

3. How frames work (and how they differ from trusses)

Wednesday introduced three structure types. Truss joint work is what you practice now; full frame analysis (cutting multi-force members and using \(\sum M\)) comes later in the term. Still, the vocabulary matters so you do not treat every pin-connected structure as a pure truss.

Structure categories
TypeMember modelTypical statics treatment
Truss Members are two-force: loads only at ends, axial force along the member Method of joints (and sections later)
Frame At least one multi-force member (three or more forces, not all concurrent or collinear) Reactions on the whole frame, then separate FBDs per member; pin forces often have two components
Machine Designed with moving parts that transmit or modify forces Same FBD ideas with kinematic links in mind

Even inside a frame, identify two-force members first. They still carry a single axial force. A pin on a multi-force bar is not automatically “one unknown at angle \(\theta\)”; you may need \(F_x\) and \(F_y\) unless you know the link is a two-force member.

Worked example: spot two-force vs multi-force

Pin-connected layout: horizontal bar \(AB\) is 2.0 m long, pinned at wall \(A\) and at \(B\). Vertical strut \(BC\) is 1.5 m long, pinned at \(B\) and at ground \(C\). A 200 N downward load acts on \(AB\) at its midpoint \(M\) (1.0 m from \(A\)).

  • Member \(BC\): forces only at the end pins \(B\) and \(C\), with no load between them → two-force. The force in \(BC\) is a single axial unknown along the strut (tension or compression).
  • Member \(AB\): pin at \(A\), the midspan load at \(M\), and the strut force at \(B\) → forces at three points → multi-force member. On this bar, a pin force is not automatically a single axial unknown; the pin at \(A\) has two components. At \(B\), once you have identified \(BC\) as two-force, the force from the strut on \(AB\) acts along \(BC\).

For global equilibrium you still find external reactions from an FBD of the entire assembly. For a full member-by-member solution you would draw \(AB\) and \(BC\) separately; that detailed frame workflow is not Wednesday’s main path, but the classification above is.

Self-check: A straight rod \(DE\) has pins only at \(D\) and \(E\) and no loads between them. Is it two-force?

AnswerYes. Only pin forces at the ends, so the rod carries a collinear axial pair of forces.

4. When the course gets harder — staying steady

One of you wrote that things make sense now but you expect to get lost when the difficulty jumps. That is a fair worry in statics: trusses lean on the same equilibrium ideas as earlier chapters, but there are more arrows on the page and more algebra chains.

  • Work a few problems across several days instead of one long cram session.
  • When a joint FBD feels messy, pause and re-label directions before you solve.
  • Ask early: Canvas, office hours, or recitation with Efe while the step is still small.
  • If you notice pattern-memorizing without knowing why an equation applies, treat that as a signal to slow down and redraw the FBD.

The next skills (sections, frames in depth, machines) all reuse \(\sum F = 0\) and \(\sum M = 0\). Solid joint bookkeeping now saves rework later.
