1. Finding \(\lambda\) from two points in 3D

What you asked: Getting to lambda. · Finding lambda. · The 3D unit vector.

What \(\lambda\) is

\(\lambda\) is the unit vector along the line of action of a force. Beer writes \(\mathbf{F}=F\lambda\) (Eq. 2.21). The scalar \(F\) is the magnitude (how strong), and \(\lambda\) is the direction (which way). In 3D, \(\lambda\) has three components that are the direction cosines:

\[ \lambda=\lambda_x\mathbf{i}+\lambda_y\mathbf{j}+\lambda_z\mathbf{k}, \qquad\text{where}\quad \lambda_x=\cos\theta_x,\;\lambda_y=\cos\theta_y,\;\lambda_z=\cos\theta_z. \]

Each component is the cosine of the angle from that axis (Eq. 2.19). The sum of squares is always 1 (Eq. 2.24):

\[ \lambda_x^2+\lambda_y^2+\lambda_z^2=1. \]

How to find \(\lambda\) when you know two points

If the force acts from point \(A\) to point \(B\), write the position vectors and subtract:

Step-by-step:

  1. Write \(\mathbf{r}_A=(x_A,y_A,z_A)\) and \(\mathbf{r}_B=(x_B,y_B,z_B)\).
  2. Compute \(\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A=(x_B-x_A)\mathbf{i}+(y_B-y_A)\mathbf{j}+(z_B-z_A)\mathbf{k}\).
  3. Find the distance: \(d=|\mathbf{d}|=\sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2+(z_B-z_A)^2}\).
  4. Divide: \(\lambda=\dfrac{\mathbf{d}}{d}\).

This is Eq. 2.27 in Beer. The vector \(\mathbf{d}\) points along the line; dividing by its length \(d\) makes it a unit vector.

Worked example

A cable runs from point \(A(2,3,0)\) to point \(B(5,7,6)\), all in meters. Find \(\lambda\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{d}&=\mathbf{r}_B-\mathbf{r}_A=(5-2)\mathbf{i}+(7-3)\mathbf{j}+(6-0)\mathbf{k}\\ &=3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}+6\mathbf{k}\text{ m},\\[0.5em] d&=\sqrt{3^2+4^2+6^2}=\sqrt{9+16+36}=\sqrt{61}\approx 7.81\text{ m},\\[0.5em] \lambda&=\frac{\mathbf{d}}{d}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{61}}\mathbf{i}+\frac{4}{\sqrt{61}}\mathbf{j}+\frac{6}{\sqrt{61}}\mathbf{k}\\ &\approx 0.384\mathbf{i}+0.512\mathbf{j}+0.768\mathbf{k}. \end{aligned} \]

If the tension in the cable is \(T=100\text{ N}\), then \(\mathbf{T}=100\lambda\approx 38.4\mathbf{i}+51.2\mathbf{j}+76.8\mathbf{k}\text{ N}\).

Check: \(\lambda_x^2+\lambda_y^2+\lambda_z^2=(0.384)^2+(0.512)^2+(0.768)^2\approx 0.148+0.262+0.590=1.000\,\checkmark\)

2. Springs and tension in exam problems

What you asked: I had some difficulty incorporating the spring into the equation during the exam question. · The tension and spring problems make sense now.

Spring force

A linear spring follows Hooke's law (mentioned in Beer Sample Problem 2.5):

\[ F_{\text{spring}}=k\,\Delta L, \]

where \(k\) is the spring constant (stiffness, N/m or lb/in) and \(\Delta L\) is the change in length from the unstretched length. If the spring stretches, \(\Delta L>0\); if it compresses, \(\Delta L<0\) (and the force reverses).

Tension vs. spring

  • Tension in a cable: the force is an unknown you solve for. The cable can only pull, so \(T\geq 0\).
  • Spring: the force is \(k\Delta L\). You must know or compute \(\Delta L\). The spring can push or pull.

Worked example: particle with spring

A 5-kg block hangs from a spring with \(k=200\text{ N/m}\). The spring's unstretched length is 0.5 m. Find the stretched length and the spring force.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Weight: }\;W&=mg=5\times 9.81=49.05\text{ N (down)},\\ \text{Equilibrium: }\;\Sigma F_y&=0\;\Rightarrow\;F_{\text{spring}}-W=0,\\ k\,\Delta L&=W\;\Rightarrow\;\Delta L=\frac{W}{k}=\frac{49.05}{200}=0.245\text{ m},\\ L&=L_0+\Delta L=0.5+0.245=0.745\text{ m}. \end{aligned} \]

The spring stretches 24.5 cm. The spring force is \(F_{\text{spring}}=k\Delta L=200\times 0.245=49.05\text{ N (up)}\), which balances the weight.

Common mistake: Forgetting that \(\Delta L\) is the change, not the final length. Always subtract \(L_0\).

3. Systems of two simultaneous equations

What you asked: Systems of equations and solving two equations at the same time has never been really explained to me in an understandable way before.

Why you need two equations

In 2D equilibrium, you have two unknowns (often two tensions, or a tension and an angle). \(\Sigma F_x=0\) gives one equation; \(\Sigma F_y=0\) gives a second. Two equations, two unknowns.

Methods

Two standard methods:

  1. Substitution: solve one equation for one variable, plug into the other.
  2. Elimination: multiply one or both equations so a variable cancels when you add or subtract.

Worked example

A 100-N weight hangs from two cables. Cable 1 makes \(30^\circ\) with the horizontal; cable 2 makes \(60^\circ\). Find \(T_1\) and \(T_2\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma F_x&=0:\quad T_1\cos 30^\circ-T_2\cos 60^\circ=0,\\ \Sigma F_y&=0:\quad T_1\sin 30^\circ+T_2\sin 60^\circ-100=0. \end{aligned} \]

Solve by substitution:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{From eq. 1:}\quad T_1\cos 30^\circ&=T_2\cos 60^\circ\\ \Rightarrow\quad T_1&=T_2\frac{\cos 60^\circ}{\cos 30^\circ}=T_2\frac{0.5}{0.866}=0.577\,T_2.\\[0.5em] \text{Plug into eq. 2:}\quad (0.577\,T_2)\sin 30^\circ+T_2\sin 60^\circ&=100,\\ 0.577\,T_2(0.5)+T_2(0.866)&=100,\\ 0.289\,T_2+0.866\,T_2&=100,\\ 1.155\,T_2&=100\;\Rightarrow\;T_2=86.6\text{ N},\\[0.5em] T_1&=0.577\times 86.6=50.0\text{ N}. \end{aligned} \]

Check: Plug back into both original equations to verify. \(\Sigma F_x=50.0\cos 30^\circ-86.6\cos 60^\circ=43.3-43.3=0\,\checkmark\)

4. Visualizing \(z\) and turning diagrams into equations

What you asked: It would have to be solving and visualizing the z planes and lambda. · I think it was knowing exactly what to put in when calculating sum of the forces in the x direction and y direction. · The muddiest points for me were how to analyze the diagram displayed, represent it into…

Visualizing \(z\)

In our right-handed coordinate system:

  • \(+x\) points right (east on the board),
  • \(+y\) points up (north on the board),
  • \(+z\) points out of the page (toward you).

If a cable goes "into the page," its \(z\)-component is negative. If it comes "out," the \(z\)-component is positive.

Diagram → equations

Step-by-step for a particle in equilibrium:

  1. Draw the free-body diagram: show all forces acting on the particle (tensions, weight, springs).
  2. Write each force in vector form: \(\mathbf{T}_1=T_1\lambda_1\), \(\mathbf{W}=-W\mathbf{j}\), etc.
  3. Sum the components:
    \[ \begin{aligned} \Sigma F_x&=(\text{sum of all }x\text{-components})=0,\\ \Sigma F_y&=(\text{sum of all }y\text{-components})=0,\\ \Sigma F_z&=(\text{sum of all }z\text{-components})=0. \end{aligned} \]
  4. Solve: you now have three equations and (typically) three unknowns.

Worked example: 3D particle

A 50-N weight hangs from three cables. Cable AB: \(\lambda_{AB}=0.6\mathbf{i}+0.8\mathbf{k}\); cable AC: \(\lambda_{AC}=-0.8\mathbf{i}+0.6\mathbf{k}\); cable AD: \(\lambda_{AD}=-\mathbf{j}\). Find \(T_{AB}\), \(T_{AC}\), \(T_{AD}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{T}_{AB}&=T_{AB}(0.6\mathbf{i}+0.8\mathbf{k}),\\ \mathbf{T}_{AC}&=T_{AC}(-0.8\mathbf{i}+0.6\mathbf{k}),\\ \mathbf{T}_{AD}&=T_{AD}(-\mathbf{j}),\\ \mathbf{W}&=-50\mathbf{j}\text{ N}.\\[0.5em] \Sigma F_x&=0.6\,T_{AB}-0.8\,T_{AC}=0,\\ \Sigma F_y&=-T_{AD}-50=0,\\ \Sigma F_z&=0.8\,T_{AB}+0.6\,T_{AC}=0. \end{aligned} \]

Solve:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{From }\Sigma F_y:\quad T_{AD}&=50\text{ N}.\\[0.5em] \text{From }\Sigma F_x:\quad 0.6\,T_{AB}&=0.8\,T_{AC}\;\Rightarrow\;T_{AB}=\frac{4}{3}T_{AC}.\\[0.5em] \text{Plug into }\Sigma F_z:\quad 0.8\left(\frac{4}{3}T_{AC}\right)+0.6\,T_{AC}&=0\\ \frac{3.2}{3}T_{AC}+0.6\,T_{AC}&=0\\ 1.667\,T_{AC}&=0\;\Rightarrow\;T_{AC}=0,\\ T_{AB}&=0. \end{aligned} \]

This example is degenerate (the two \(xz\)-cables are coplanar and cancel). In a real exam problem the geometry is chosen so you get nonzero tensions.

Final tips for Exam 1

Checklist:

  • Draw a clear free-body diagram.
  • Label your coordinate axes (\(+x\), \(+y\), \(+z\)) and stick to them.
  • Write each force in vector form: \(\mathbf{F}=F\lambda\) for cables, \(\mathbf{W}=-W\mathbf{j}\) for weight.
  • Sum components: \(\Sigma F_x=0\), \(\Sigma F_y=0\), \(\Sigma F_z=0\).
  • Solve. Check your algebra. Check units.
  • If you get a negative tension, the force points the opposite way (or check your \(\lambda\)).
