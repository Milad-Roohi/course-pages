1. Recipe: centroid of an area by integration

Fix a region in the \(xy\) plane with uniform material. The centroid \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) is the single point that summarizes the geometry of the area:

\[ A = \int dA \qquad \bar{x} = \frac{\displaystyle\int x_{\mathrm{el}}\,dA}{\displaystyle A} \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\displaystyle\int y_{\mathrm{el}}\,dA}{\displaystyle A} \]

Those three formulas use one choice of area element \(dA\). \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\) are not two different “methods”; they are two coordinates of the same point. You do not set up one integral “for \(\bar{x}\)” and a separate rule “for \(\bar{y}\).” You pick \(dA\), identify \((x_{\mathrm{el}}, y_{\mathrm{el}})\) for that strip, and use both \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\) formulas with the same \(dA\) and limits.

First moments link directly: \(Q_y = \int x_{\mathrm{el}}\,dA = \bar{x}A\) and \(Q_x = \int y_{\mathrm{el}}\,dA = \bar{y}A\).

Decision recipe

  1. Sketch the region and label the boundary curves. Mark a typical strip.
  2. Choose the element: vertical strip (\(dA = y\,dx\) if the strip runs from the \(x\)-axis up to the top curve) or horizontal strip (\(dA = x\,dy\) if the strip runs from the \(y\)-axis to the right boundary). Pick whichever makes one integral over the natural coordinate—or a single split at a kink—not two unrelated pieces.
  3. Write \(dA\) using the height or width of your strip, expressed in the integration variable.
  4. Element centroid: for a vertical strip from \(y=0\) to \(y=y(x)\), \(x_{\mathrm{el}} = x\) and \(y_{\mathrm{el}} = y(x)/2\). A common mistake is using \(y_{\mathrm{el}} = y(x)\) instead of \(y(x)/2\). For a horizontal strip at height \(y\) with width from the left boundary to the right boundary, \(y_{\mathrm{el}} = y\) and \(x_{\mathrm{el}}\) is at the midpoint of that width.
  5. Limits come from the sketch: the smallest and largest values of the integration variable that sweep the whole region.
  6. Integrate for \(A\), then \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\). Check units (area in m\(^2\) implies lengths in meters).

Vertical vs horizontal strips. For a region under one curve \(y = f(x)\) from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\), a vertical strip is usually the fastest path. If the right boundary is a vertical line \(x=b\) and the top is \(y=f(x)\), a horizontal strip needs \(x\) from the curve \(x=g(y)\) to \(x=b\); that is still fine when the top and bottom boundaries are easier to read in \(y\). Either strip choice must give the same \(A\), \(\bar{x}\), and \(\bar{y}\) when set up correctly.

2. Example 1: area under a parabola (vertical and horizontal strips)

All lengths in meters. The region lies in the first quadrant, bounded below by the \(x\)-axis, on the right by the line \(x=1\), and above by \(y = 4x^2\). The sketch is a parabolic cap clipped at \(x=1\) (where \(y=4\) m).

Vertical strip

At position \(x\), the strip runs from \(y=0\) to \(y=4x^2\). Then \(dA = 4x^2\,dx\), \(x_{\mathrm{el}} = x\), and \(y_{\mathrm{el}} = 2x^2\).

\[ A = \int_0^1 4x^2\,dx = \frac{4}{3}\ \text{m}^2 \]
\[ \bar{x} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 x\cdot 4x^2\,dx}{A} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 4x^3\,dx}{4/3} = \frac{3}{4}\ \text{m} \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 (2x^2)\cdot 4x^2\,dx}{A} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 8x^4\,dx}{4/3} = \frac{6}{5} = 1.20\ \text{m} \]

Horizontal strip (check on \(\bar{y}\))

For height \(y\) between \(0\) and \(4\), the region spans horizontally from the parabola \(x = \sqrt{y/4}\) to the line \(x=1\). The strip width is \(1 - \sqrt{y/4}\); \(dA = \bigl(1 - \tfrac{\sqrt{y}}{2}\bigr)\,dy\) and \(y_{\mathrm{el}} = y\).

\[ A = \int_0^4 \left(1 - \frac{\sqrt{y}}{2}\right)dy = \frac{4}{3}\ \text{m}^2 \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^4 y\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt{y}}{2}\right)dy}{A} = 1.20\ \text{m} \]

The horizontal setup reproduces the same area and \(\bar{y}\) as the vertical strip—good evidence that \(y_{\mathrm{el}}\) and the limits were chosen correctly.

Sanity: \(\bar{x}=0.75\) m is between \(0\) and \(1\); \(\bar{y}=1.20\) m is below the top of the region at \(y=4\) m and above mid-height of the “bulk” of the area, which sits nearer the \(x\)-axis because the parabola is flat there.

3. Example 2: centroid of a wire along a circular arc (\(L\) vs \(A\))

A thin wire follows a quarter circle of radius \(R = 3\) m centered at the origin, from \((3,0)\) to \((0,3)\). Use arc length \(dL\), not area \(dA\):

\[ L = \int dL \qquad \bar{x} = \frac{\displaystyle\int x\,dL}{L} \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\displaystyle\int y\,dL}{L} \]

With \(x = R\cos\theta\), \(y = R\sin\theta\), and \(dL = R\,d\theta\) for \(\theta\) from \(0\) to \(\pi/2\):

\[ L = \int_0^{\pi/2} R\,d\theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}\ \text{m} \qquad \bar{x} = \bar{y} = \frac{R^2\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\,d\theta}{L} = \frac{6}{\pi} \approx 1.91\ \text{m} \]

\(L\) vs \(A\) in one line: line or wire problems integrate \(dL\) and report total length \(L\); area problems integrate \(dA\) and report \(A\). Centroid-of-volume uses \(dV\) and \(V\). The integral skeleton is always “first moment divided by total size,” but the element and symbol change with the geometry type.

4. Example 3: area between two curves

In meters, find the centroid of the region between \(y = x\) and \(y = x^2\) in the first quadrant. Intersections: \(x = x^2\) gives \(x=0\) and \(x=1\).

Vertical strip at \(x\): height \(h(x) = x - x^2\), so \(dA = (x-x^2)\,dx\), \(x_{\mathrm{el}} = x\), and \(y_{\mathrm{el}} = \dfrac{x + x^2}{2}\).

\[ A = \int_0^1 (x-x^2)\,dx = \frac{1}{6}\ \text{m}^2 \qquad \bar{x} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 x(x-x^2)\,dx}{A} = \frac{1}{2}\ \text{m} \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 \tfrac{x+x^2}{2}(x-x^2)\,dx}{A} = 0.40\ \text{m} \]

The limits came straight from intersection points—always a reliable way to pin \(x\) or \(y\) when two curves bound the region.

5. Notation, concepts, and exams

  • Centroid vs center of mass vs center of gravity. For a uniform plate in a uniform gravity field, all three points coincide. The centroid is purely geometric (\(\int dA\), \(\int x\,dA\), …). Center of mass weights by density; center of gravity weights by \(g\) (or \(\rho g\)). When \(\rho\) and \(g\) are constant, the coordinates match the centroid.
  • Tables vs integration. Composite shapes from rectangles and circles still use the table method from earlier in the chapter. Integration is for curved boundaries or layouts that do not split cleanly into standard parts.
  • Exams. Be ready both to set up \(\int x_{\mathrm{el}}\,dA\) and similar integrals in symbols and to evaluate them numerically when arithmetic is part of the question. We are not committing here to a single exam format beyond that.

6. Practice (self-check)

Try these without looking at the earlier examples; click the answers when you are ready.

Problem A. Quarter circular disk of radius \(2\) m in the first quadrant (center at origin, bounded by axes and the arc \(x^2+y^2=4\)). Find \(A\) and \(\bar{x}\) (by symmetry, \(\bar{y}=\bar{x}\)).

Answer

\(A = \pi R^2/4 = \pi\ \text{m}^2\). In polar form, \(\bar{x} = \dfrac{1}{A}\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^2 r\cos\theta\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \dfrac{8}{3\pi} \approx 0.849\) m (and the same for \(\bar{y}\)).

Problem B. Straight wire from \((0,0)\) to \((3,4)\) m. Find \(L\) and the centroid of the wire.

Answer

\(L = 5\) m. A uniform straight segment has its centroid at the midpoint: \((\bar{x},\bar{y}) = (1.50,\,2.00)\) m.

Problem C. Region under \(y = 2x\) from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\) m ( \(x\)-axis below). Set up—do not evaluate—\(A\), \(\bar{x}\), and \(\bar{y}\) using a vertical strip and correct \(y_{\mathrm{el}}\).

Answer

\(dA = 2x\,dx\), \(x_{\mathrm{el}} = x\), \(y_{\mathrm{el}} = x\) (half the strip height \(y=2x\)). Then \(A = \int_0^2 2x\,dx\), \(\bar{x} = \dfrac{\int_0^2 x\cdot 2x\,dx}{A}\), \(\bar{y} = \dfrac{\int_0^2 x\cdot 2x\,dx}{A}\). (Evaluating gives \(A=4\) m\(^2\), \(\bar{x}=\bar{y}=4/3\) m.)

Definitions worth keeping straight

  • Area element \(dA\). Product of strip thickness and strip height/width; limits from the sketch.
  • Line element \(dL\). Arc length along a curve—often \(dL = \sqrt{(dx/d\theta)^2+(dy/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta\) or \(R\,d\theta\) for a circle.
  • Element centroid \((x_{\mathrm{el}}, y_{\mathrm{el}})\). Centroid of the thin strip itself, not necessarily on the boundary curve.
  • Same point. \(\bar{x}\) and \(\bar{y}\) from the same \(dA\) describe one centroid.
